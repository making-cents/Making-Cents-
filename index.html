FAQs
Do I need any prior knowledge of economics to use this?+
No. Every concept is written from first principles. If a term requires understanding a previous concept, that concept is explained first. You can begin anywhere in the course and follow the links where needed.
Who is this course designed for?+
Making Cents is written primarily for readers aged 13 to 15, though the content is accessible to anyone who wants a plain-English introduction to economics. The concepts are the same ones discussed in news coverage and public debate — explained without assumption of prior knowledge.
How long does each lesson take?+
Most lessons are designed to be read in three to five minutes. The goal is to explain one concept clearly and completely — not to be comprehensive at the expense of clarity.
Can I suggest a concept to be covered?+
Yes. If there is an economic term or idea you have encountered and would like explained, use the Contact page to submit a suggestion. Reader suggestions directly inform what gets covered next.

Who we are

Economics is present in almost every significant decision a society makes, yet for most young people, the subject remains inaccessible: either too academic in its framing, or too simplified to be genuinely useful.

Making Cents was founded on a straightforward premise: that a young teenager who hears the word "recession" on the news deserves a clear, honest account of what it means — rather than the usual dismissal, or a textbook definition stripped of context.

The goal is not to produce economists. It is to produce citizens who understand the forces shaping their lives.

About me

Dinner has always held a specific significance in our house. It was not only a time for resting and eating together as a family, but it also presented an opportunity to share and talk about the things we had learned or seen that day that truly interested us. With a sister four years my senior and two parents who were both lively and deeply knowledgeable about the world, our dinner table was always rife with debate and discussion about current affairs, sports, politics, and history, to name just a few topics. However, as my sister grew a bit older, a common topic of discussion became increasingly central to our conversations, one which I neither understood nor cared for: economics. My lack of appreciation was most likely rooted in my lack of comprehension, and to this day, I still regret not speaking up earlier to ask what economics really meant before deciding to disregard it. Economics shapes so much of the world around us — wages, rent, opportunity, and political decisions — and yet it is rarely explained to the people most affected by it: future generations.

This platform exists to change that. Every concept is written assuming no prior knowledge and is honest about where genuine uncertainty or disagreement exists among economists. The ultimate aim is clarity, offering a first insight for those who are curious about understanding economics. If I were to leave you with just one thing after these lessons, it would be the confidence to speak up. Whether it is at the dinner table, in the classroom, or just among friends, my goal is to empower you to fluently discuss and understand the science that shapes the world around us.

George Poliakov

What we stand for

I

Plain language, always.

Technical vocabulary is used only where it is the most precise available tool — and always explained on first use. Complexity is never a substitute for clarity.

II

Grounded in the familiar.

Every concept is introduced through something the reader already has experience of — a price, a pay slip, a headline. Abstraction follows, not precedes, understanding.

III

Honest about uncertainty.

Economics contains genuine disputes. Where the evidence is contested or incomplete, this platform says so — and explains why reasonable people disagree.

Why use Making Cents?

When a chatbot can answer almost anything, why learn from a platform like this?

Katherine Rundell, an English author and academic, declared her opposition to AI’s effects on the younger generation, believing that “It risks becoming an iron curtain that comes down between a young person and their potential for thinking, for experiment and conquering frustration, for stamina and for invention”. Whilst I completely understand the concern voiced by many educators regarding AI’s potential to significantly hinder the critical thinking skills and development of younger people, it is perhaps important that we also remind ourselves of a similar objection being held against the introduction of calculators in the 1960s on the basis that they would erode foundational numeracy skills. I am not trying to compare the invention of the high school calculator to modern AI systems, nor am I saying we shouldn’t be careful of how we integrate the use of AI into the school system, but I believe the comparison highlights how artificial intelligence simply represents the next logical step in human innovation: another major technological shift, much like the industrial, electrical, and internet revolutions before it, that will fundamentally restructure how we work and live.

I often use AI myself. It can help me organise my schedule, structure my revision, or explain a topic when I cannot quite understand it. Used in this way, it can be an incredibly useful tool. But this raises a more difficult question: where do we draw the line? At what point does using AI to help us complete a task become relying on AI to complete the thinking for us?

These questions are particularly relevant to education. If an AI system can explain almost any economic concept in seconds, why should you spend time reading a platform such as Making Cents at all? Making Cents does not serve as a similar resource to something like a dense textbook, or a series of hour-long, undergraduate level lectures, so what can a website like this offer that a chatbot cannot?

In reality, the answer to the question I have posed is, at a surface level, quite obvious; AI can already do much of what Making Cents attempts to do. Ask a chatbot why inflation occurs, why house prices are so high, or why some countries are richer than others, and within seconds you can receive a detailed explanation. So why spend time reading an article about it? If I had to give just one overarching answer, it would be because learning is not simply the process of obtaining information, nor is it about reading a huge block of text, compiled from numerous unknown sources, smushed together and compressed into an explanation seemingly dressed up as the “answer”. There is an important distinction between being given an answer and understanding why that answer is true. Economics is not simply a collection of definitions and facts that we need to memorise. It truly is a way of thinking about the world: questioning why things happen, considering the incentives facing different people, examining evidence, recognising trade-offs and, sometimes, discovering that there is no simple answer at all.

This is where I think the way we use AI becomes important. If I am struggling to understand a concept and ask AI to explain it in a different way, it can be an incredibly effective learning tool. But if I immediately turn to AI whenever I encounter something difficult, I risk removing the very process through which understanding develops. I am not going to claim that Making Cents can explain economics better than an AI system in every circumstance. Nor do I think it should try to. Instead, Making Cents is intended to give you the opportunity to slow down and properly interact with the ideas you are learning. Every lesson is carefully curated, the information is rigorously checked, and our sources are available for you to explore further. More importantly, we give you the opportunity to actively connect with what you have learned. You can explore a question and test yourself with our in-built quizzes, rather than giving you an explanation filled with jargon and complex terminology.

Our aim, therefore, is for you to engage with the content, make mistakes, try again, and ultimately learn from your previous errors. Learning through failure is not only rewarding, but can also help us develop a deeper understanding and stronger memory of what we have learned. Becoming comfortable with failure, a sensation which AI will often not provide you with, is invaluable; almost any meaningful achievement in life involves getting things wrong before getting them right.

Bill Gates once said that “it’s fine to celebrate success, but it is more important to heed the lessons of failure”, and while Making Cents is perhaps not quite on the same level as Microsoft yet, it is through this central philosophy that we hope to make learning economics a more fun, and importantly, meaningful experience.
